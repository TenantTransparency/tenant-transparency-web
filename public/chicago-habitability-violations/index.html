What Counts as a Habitability Violation in Chicago

Updated for 2026 · Part of the Chicago Renters' Rights series

This is not legal advice. This article explains general rules under the Chicago RLTO for informational purposes only. Withholding rent or making repair-and-deduct decisions incorrectly can expose you to an eviction claim, so if you're facing a serious habitability issue, consult a licensed Illinois attorney or a tenant rights organization before acting.

At a glance

  • Landlords must keep a unit code compliant and fit to live in. That includes heat, water, plumbing, electrical systems, structure, locks and pests.
  • For ordinary repairs, you give written notice and 14 days. For essential services like heat and water, the City describes a faster track.
  • Repair-and-deduct is capped at the greater of $500 or half a month's rent, and the City's summary adds that it cannot exceed one month's rent. You must give the landlord receipts.
  • Withholding rent without following the process can expose you to an eviction case. Follow the notice steps and keep records.

1. What counts as a violation

RLTO Section 5-12-110 defines a habitability problem broadly. It covers a landlord's failure to:

  • Comply with applicable building code requirements
  • Maintain and repair equipment the landlord supplies, such as heating systems, plumbing fixtures and appliances included in the lease
  • Keep the dwelling unit and common areas in a fit and habitable condition

In practice, that reaches a wide range of conditions. Here is how common problems tend to line up:

If something falls in the gray zone, document it and send a written notice anyway. A notice costs nothing, and it starts the clock.

2. Two tracks: repairs and essential services

The City's plain-language summary of the RLTO describes two different paths, depending on what is wrong.

Minor defects and repairsEssential services
ExamplesLeaky faucet, broken cabinet, failed appliance, damaged doorHeat, running water, hot water, electricity, gas
First stepWritten repair requestWritten notice
Wait period14 days without repairsThe summary describes requesting correction within 24 hours
Then you mayHave the work done and deduct the cost, within the cap, with code-compliant work and receipts to the landlordHave the service restored and deduct the cost with paid receipts, or withhold rent after requesting the 24-hour correction

The faster track exists because living without heat or water is a safety issue. Even so, use it carefully. Withholding rent is the riskiest remedy, and a mistake can give a landlord a nonpayment argument. If heat is out in cold weather, call 311 first, then send your written notice the same day. Both create records.

3. The 14-day notice requirement

Before you use a remedy for an ordinary defect, you generally need to give the landlord written notice specifying the exact condition, and a reasonable opportunity to fix it. The ordinance sets this at 14 days for most repair and withholding remedies. If you are moving toward lease termination for a serious violation, the notice has to state that the agreement will terminate in not less than 14 days unless the landlord fixes the problem.

The clock starts when the landlord actually receives your notice, not when you send it. Certified mail with a return receipt, or another method that documents delivery, protects you. Email is useful as a second copy, but do not rely on it alone.

Counting the 14 days Taylor sends a written notice about a leaking kitchen sink by certified mail on March 3. The return receipt shows delivery on March 6. The 14 days run from March 6, so the landlord's window ends on March 20. Taylor waits until after that date before using any remedy, and calendars March 21 as the first day to act.

6. Writing a notice that works

The generator above produces a solid starting letter. A few details separate a notice that helps you from one that does not:

  • Be specific. "The kitchen sink under-cabinet pipe has leaked since February 10, causing water damage to the cabinet floor" is better than "plumbing problems."
  • State dates. When it started, when you first reported it, and every earlier attempt to get it fixed.
  • Attach photos. Show the condition and the date.
  • Offer access. Give the landlord times they can come, and ask for notice of any entry. See our entry rules guide.
  • Send it so delivery is provable. Certified mail with a return receipt, plus a copy by email.
  • Keep your own copy, the receipt and a log of every reply.
You are protected for asking Asking for required repairs, and complaining to the City, are activities the RLTO protects from retaliation. If your landlord responds with a rent increase, a non-renewal or an eviction threat, see our guide to retaliation and the one-year presumption.

7. Choosing a remedy

One repair, cost under the cap, landlord has gone quietRepair-and-deduct after 14 daysWork that is not code compliant, or missing receipts
A condition continues and cannot be fixed by one repairRent withholding in a defensible amountWithholding too much, or too early
Heat, water or another essential service is outThe essential services track, with a 311 reportSkipping written notice
The unit is unsafe and the landlord will not actTermination notice, ideally with legal adviceMoving out without a proper notice and proof
You want the building on record311 or Department of Buildings complaintNone, and it does not replace written notice to the landlord

Withholding rent comes with an extra consideration in Chicago. The standard lease says that if you withhold more rent than the law allows and are sued, the excess must be deposited with the court. That makes accuracy matter: withhold a defensible amount, document why, and keep records.

8. Worked examples

A $300 leak repair on $1,500 rent Jordan sends a written notice about a leaking pipe and gets no response in 14 days. The cap on repair-and-deduct is the greater of $500 or half of $1,500, which is $750, and it cannot exceed one month's rent. A licensed plumber quotes $300, within the cap. Jordan has the repair done, keeps the receipt and gives the landlord a copy, then deducts $300 from the next rent payment with a short written explanation.
No heat in January The boiler fails on a cold night. Sam calls 311 the same day to request an inspection, then sends a written notice to the landlord with the date, time and indoor temperature readings from the heat tool. This is an essential service, so Sam uses the faster track the City's summary describes and documents everything. If heat is not restored, Sam talks to a tenant rights organization about the next step, rather than guessing.
A persistent leak and mold A roof leak keeps causing mold in a bedroom. Alex sends notice, the landlord patches it twice, and it returns. The bedroom is unusable for six weeks. Alex documents the dates, takes photos and keeps a diary of when the room could not be used. A defensible rent reduction might reflect the share of the home that was lost, for example a bedroom out of three rooms, for the weeks it was unusable. There is no fixed formula, so Alex consults an attorney before withholding and records the reasoning.

9. Getting the City involved

A written notice to your landlord and a complaint to the City do different jobs. The notice starts the clock for your remedies. The City complaint puts an independent inspector in the unit and creates a public record.

  • Call 311 or file online to request a building inspection. Describe the condition and give your address and unit.
  • Be present or give access for the inspection, and ask for the inspection result in writing.
  • Keep the violation notice, which shows what the City found and any deadlines it gave the landlord.
  • Check the building's record on our property search to see violation history that others have faced there.
  • Tell us what happened. You can add a moderated report through Report an Issue, which helps future renters.

10. Dollar limits and what can go wrong

  • Repair-and-deduct cap: the greater of $500 or half a month's rent, not exceeding one month's rent. Going over that amount without a clear basis risks the deduction being challenged.
  • Withholding without notice: the most common mistake. Withholding rent before giving proper written notice and the 14-day window can expose you to a nonpayment eviction, since the landlord may not know the withholding is protected.
  • No documentation: photos, dated written notice and records of landlord communication are what make these remedies defensible in court.
  • Work that is not up to code: a repair you arrange has to comply with the building code. Use a qualified professional, not a favor from a friend, and keep the invoice.
  • Missing receipts: give the landlord copies of the receipts for any repair you deduct.
  • Utility outages beyond the landlord's control: if a utility provider itself cannot provide service, withholding and termination remedies generally do not apply to that specific failure.
  • Caused by the tenant: the landlord may charge you for problems you or your guests caused, so do not use these remedies for those.

12. FAQ

What counts as a habitability violation?

Building code noncompliance, failure to maintain supplied equipment, or failure to keep the unit fit and habitable, such as no heat, broken plumbing, structural hazards or pest infestations.

How much notice do I have to give before withholding rent?

Generally 14 days of written notice describing the specific problem, given before you withhold or use repair-and-deduct. The City's summary describes a faster track for essential services.

Is there a dollar limit on repair-and-deduct?

Yes. The greater of $500 or half a month's rent, and the City's summary adds that it cannot exceed one month's rent. You must give the landlord receipts.

Can I just stop paying rent if my landlord won't fix something?

Not safely. You need to follow the notice-and-cure process first, or you risk a nonpayment eviction claim.

Does it matter how I send the notice?

Yes. The clock starts when the landlord receives it, so use certified mail with a return receipt, and send an email copy as well.

Can my landlord retaliate if I ask for repairs?

Retaliation for requesting required repairs or complaining to the City is prohibited, and protected activity within a year before a landlord's act creates a presumption of retaliation. See our retaliation guide.

What if the landlord says I caused the problem?

Landlords can charge for tenant-caused damage. Your photos and your move-in record help show what was there before. Dated documentation of when the problem began matters most.
