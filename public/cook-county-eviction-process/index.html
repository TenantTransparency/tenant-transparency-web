Chicago Renters' Rights: The Complete Guide  /  Eviction

How Eviction Actually Works in Cook County

Updated for 2026 · Part of the Chicago Renters' Rights series

This is not legal advice. This article explains the general eviction process for informational purposes only. If you've received a notice or an eviction filing, contact a licensed Illinois attorney or a tenant rights organization immediately. Deadlines in eviction cases move fast and missing one can cost you the case.

The short version

  • A landlord cannot lock you out, remove your belongings or cut your utilities. Only a court order, carried out by the Cook County Sheriff, can remove you.
  • The notice comes first: 5 days for unpaid rent, 10 days for a lease violation, 30, 60 or 120 days for a no-cause termination, non-renewal or rent increase in Chicago.
  • An expired notice is not an eviction. The landlord still has to file, serve you and win.
  • If you are served with court papers, go to the court date. If you do not, the landlord may get a default judgment.
  • Free legal help and court-based rental assistance exist. Ask early.

1. Rule one: no self-help evictions

A Chicago landlord generally cannot legally remove you by changing the locks, taking your belongings, shutting off utilities, or simply telling you to leave. Evictions generally have to go through Cook County court and end in a court order before a sheriff (not the landlord, not a property manager, not a private security guard) can remove you.

Under the City's RLTO summary, a landlord who locks out a tenant can be fined $200 to $500 for each day the lockout occurs or continues. If a landlord has locked you out or cut service, document it, call 311, and call 911 if you are in danger. Our heat and utilities guide covers utility shutoffs in more detail.

A few things that are not an eviction, so you know the difference:

  • A text, call or verbal statement that you have to leave.
  • A notice taped to the door. A notice starts a clock. It does not remove you.
  • A letter from a lawyer or a "pay or quit" threat.
  • Someone knocking and saying they are there to "enforce" something without a court order.

2. The notice types

NoticeUsed forLength
5-Day NoticeNonpayment of rent5 days
10-Day NoticeLease violation (unauthorized occupant, pet, damage, etc.)10 days
Fair Notice tiers (30/60/120-day)Ending tenancy without cause, non-renewal, rent increase30, 60, or 120 days based on tenancy length

The 5-day and 10-day notices are unaffected by how long you've lived in the unit. The tiered Fair Notice periods only apply when the landlord isn't citing nonpayment, a lease violation, disturbance of others, or abandonment.

The 5-day notice for unpaid rent

A 5-day notice demands the rent and gives you 5 days to pay or move out. Most commonly cited elements of a valid notice are the date, the name of each tenant, the address including the unit, the exact amount owed, the period it covers, a demand to pay or leave within 5 days, where to pay, the landlord's name and address, and a signature. If you pay the full amount demanded within the period, the landlord generally cannot file. Count calendar days, weekends included, and do not count the day you were served. Keep proof of every payment, and ask for a receipt.

The 10-day notice for a lease violation

A 10-day notice describes something the landlord says you did or did not do, such as having an unauthorized occupant, keeping a pet the lease forbids, or damaging the unit. In many Cook County cases, the tenant has 10 days to cure the violation. If you can fix the issue, do so and document it with dated photos, receipts or a written confirmation. If you think the claim is wrong, write back and say so, and keep records.

No-cause notices

If you are current on rent and have not violated the lease, a landlord who wants you out, wants to decline renewal, or wants to raise the rent must give a Fair Notice period (next section). A landlord also generally cannot end a fixed-term lease early without cause unless the lease allows it.

3. The Fair Notice Ordinance tiers

Chicago's Fair Notice Ordinance (Municipal Code 5-12-130) requires longer notice the longer you've lived somewhere:

How long you have lived thereNotice required
Under 6 months30 days
6 months to 3 years60 days
More than 3 years120 days

This applies to ending a tenancy without cause, declining to renew, and raising the rent. If the landlord serves a shorter notice than your tenancy length requires, you generally have the right to stay for the full required period, or pay the prior rent for that period. The defective notice doesn't shorten your time, it usually just starts a longer clock. Owner-occupied buildings of six units or fewer are exempt from these tiers, similar to the general RLTO exemption.

If you get a rent increase notice, read it for two things: whether it gives you the full tier, and whether the new rent starts only after that period ends. A rent increase that takes effect early is the kind of defect worth raising in writing.

5. Was the notice served correctly?

A notice only counts if it was delivered in a way the law accepts. Commonly accepted methods are hand delivery to the tenant, hand delivery to a person at least 13 years old who lives at the property, or certified or registered mail with a return receipt. Taping a notice to the door is generally only accepted when the property is vacant. A faulty or improperly served notice has to be redone and re-served, and a landlord proves service with an affidavit of service.

Defects worth noticing include:

  • Wrong notice for the situation (a 5-day notice used for a lease violation, for example).
  • A period that is too short for the type of notice or the Fair Notice tier.
  • Missing tenant names, address or unit, amount owed, or signature.
  • Served by regular mail only, or only posted, when you live there.
  • A notice that demands more than you actually owe, such as unrelated fees mixed into the rent figure.

Spotting a defect is useful in court. It is not a reason to ignore the notice. If you can safely resolve the problem, do. If you cannot, keep the notice, the envelope, and a note of how and when you got it.

6. Three worked examples

Example 1: unpaid rent

Ana's rent of $1,350 was due October 1 and she has not paid. On Thursday, October 8, 2026, her landlord hands her a 5-day notice for $1,350. The day of service is not counted, so the five days are October 9, 10, 11, 12 and 13. The period ends Tuesday, October 13, and the landlord generally cannot file before Wednesday, October 14.

If Ana pays the full $1,350 by October 13, gets a receipt, and keeps proof, the landlord generally cannot file on that notice. If she can only pay part, she should call a legal aid organization before anyone accepts a partial payment, because what a landlord does with a partial payment can matter later.

Example 2: a no-cause non-renewal

Dev has lived in his apartment for 2 years and is current on rent. His lease runs month to month. On March 15, 2027, his landlord serves a 30-day notice saying the tenancy ends April 14. Dev has lived there between 6 months and 3 years, so the Fair Notice tier is 60 days. Sixty days from March 15 is May 14, 2027. Because the notice was too short, Dev generally has the right to stay through the full 60-day period, or pay the prior rent for it. If the landlord files before then, the notice defect is a defense.

Example 3: a lease violation

Mei's lease bans pets and a 10-day notice says there is a cat in her unit. She has 10 days from service to cure. If she rehomes the cat and sends the landlord a dated written confirmation with a photo of the empty unit or a note from the new owner, she has documentation of the cure. If she keeps the cat, the landlord may move forward after the 10 days. Whether the violation is real, serious, or waived by earlier tolerance is the kind of question to bring to a lawyer.

7. What happens in court

  1. Notice period expires. Only after this can the landlord file.
  2. Landlord files an eviction case in Cook County court with a complaint, the notice and proof of service, a copy of the lease (or an explanation if there is none), and a summons. The plaintiff should be the property owner. A property manager cannot file the case on their own.
  3. You are served. In Cook County the Sheriff makes the first attempt to serve the summons. The summons sets your first court date and the return date is set at least 7 days after service. Under a Supreme Court order, residential eviction summonses include a notice about court-based rental assistance.
  4. Court date. You can appear and raise defenses (see below), negotiate, or in some cases request a jury trial. Many Cook County eviction hearings have been held by video, so read your summons for how and where to appear.
  5. Judgment. If the landlord wins, the court issues an eviction order. If you did not appear after being served, the landlord may get a default judgment.
  6. Sheriff enforcement. Only the Cook County Sheriff can physically carry out an eviction order. This is scheduled separately and takes time.

8. If you are served with court papers

  1. Read the summons for the court date and how to appear. Write the date everywhere you will see it. That date is the one deadline you cannot miss.
  2. Get help right away. Use the free resources below. Do not wait until the week of the hearing.
  3. Gather your documents: lease, notice and envelope, rent receipts or bank records, texts and emails with the landlord, photos of any repair problems, your 311 or inspection records.
  4. Ask about rental assistance. If the case is about unpaid rent, a court-based rental assistance program may be able to pay what you owe. Ask legal aid or the court staff whether it is available.
  5. Show up, on time. If you cannot attend, contact the court or a legal aid organization before the date to ask what to do, rather than simply not appearing.
  6. Do not leave without checking. Moving out because of a notice or a filing can end your ability to raise defenses or negotiate. Talk to a lawyer first.

If you miss the hearing, the landlord may obtain a default judgment. A tenant typically has about 30 days after an order is entered to ask the court to vacate a default, but do not rely on that window. Call legal aid immediately if you missed a date.

9. After judgment: the order and the sheriff

A judgment does not mean you are out the next day. A landlord with an eviction order generally has to ask the Sheriff to schedule enforcement, and in Cook County the landlord may need to submit a verification about rental assistance before the Sheriff will schedule it. The Sheriff's office does not enforce eviction orders on some dates, such as in extreme weather. Check the Cook County Sheriff's current schedule and notices.

The court can also give a stay, meaning extra time to move, especially when the parties agree. If you have lost and cannot stay, use the time before enforcement to find housing, document the unit's condition on move-out (see our move-out documentation guide), and claim your deposit rights (see our deposit law guide).

10. Common defenses

  • Defective notice. Wrong notice type, wrong length, missing required details (full address, amount owed, signature), or served incorrectly.
  • Retaliation. If the eviction follows soon after a building code complaint or organizing activity, Chicago law presumes retaliation unless the landlord rebuts it. The presumption applies to certain landlord actions within one year of a protected act. See our entry and retaliation guide.
  • Habitability / conditions. In some nonpayment cases, unresolved serious repair issues can be raised as a defense or offset. See our habitability guide.
  • Partial payment accepted after a 5-day notice. Depending on the circumstances, this can be argued to waive the notice, requiring the landlord to start over.
  • Payment or cure. You paid or fixed the problem within the notice period. Proof is everything: receipts, bank records, photos.
  • The landlord does not own or cannot show standing. A property manager is not the plaintiff, and an LLC owner generally needs a lawyer to appear for it.

These are general categories, not guarantees. Whether any defense applies depends on your specific facts and documentation, which is exactly the kind of thing to bring to an attorney or a court-based legal aid table before your hearing.

11. Settling a case

Many eviction cases end with an agreement rather than a trial. Courts can allow extra time to access rental assistance, legal aid and mediation, and approved statewide agreed orders exist to help parties resolve cases. A settlement can include a payment plan, more time to move, a waived late fee, or an agreement about what the record says. Before you sign anything:

  • Read it completely, including the part about what happens if you miss a date. Some agreed orders let the landlord get an eviction order immediately.
  • Make sure the amount matches your records.
  • Get a copy.
  • Ask a legal aid lawyer to look at it first if you can.

An eviction filing can appear on tenant screening reports even when the case is later dismissed. Ask a legal aid organization about options such as dismissal terms and sealing.

14. FAQ

Can my landlord evict me without going to court?

Generally no. Self-help evictions are generally not permitted. A court order is generally required before a sheriff can remove you.

How much notice does my landlord have to give?

Depends on the reason: 5 days for nonpayment, 10 days for a lease violation, or 30/60/120 days (based on tenancy length) for a no-cause termination, non-renewal, or rent increase.

What if my landlord doesn't give the correct notice period?

You generally get to stay for the full required period, or pay the prior rent for that period, even though a shorter notice was served.

How long does an eviction case take?

Varies with court load and whether the case is contested, but it generally runs through filing, a court date, judgment, and separate sheriff enforcement rather than a same-day removal.

What if I miss my court date?

The landlord may get a default judgment. Contact a legal aid organization immediately. A tenant typically has about 30 days after an order is entered to ask to vacate a default, but it is not guaranteed.

Can I stop an eviction by paying?

For unpaid rent, paying the full amount demanded within the notice period generally prevents a filing. After a case is filed, the outcome depends on the court, the landlord and any rental assistance or agreed order.

Does an expired notice mean I have to leave?

No. It means the landlord can start a court case. You do not have to leave until a court order is obtained and enforced by the sheriff.

Where can I get free help?

Eviction Help Illinois (evictionhelpillinois.org, 855-631-0811) and Cook County Legal Aid for Housing and Debt (cookcountylegalaid.org, 855-956-5763) are good places to start.
