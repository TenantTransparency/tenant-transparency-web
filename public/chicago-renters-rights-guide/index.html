Chicago Renters' Rights: The Complete Guide

Updated for 2026 · Plain-language guide to the Chicago Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance (RLTO)

This is not legal advice. This article explains general rules under the Chicago RLTO for informational purposes only. Laws change, exceptions apply, and every lease and situation is different. If you're facing an eviction, a denied deposit return, or a habitability dispute, consult a licensed Illinois attorney or a tenant rights organization before acting.

2. Who the RLTO covers

The Chicago Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance (RLTO) sets the baseline rules for most residential leases inside city limits. It covers things state law doesn't reach in the same detail: security deposit handling, notice requirements, habitability remedies, and tenant remedies for landlord violations.

CoveredGenerally not covered
Apartments and houses rented from a landlord who owns more than 6 units, or doesn't live in the buildingOwner-occupied buildings of 6 units or fewer where the owner lives on-site
Most condos and single-family homes rented out by an owner who doesn't live thereHotel and motel rooms rented short-term
Subsidized housing, with some RLTO provisions layered on top of federal rulesSome student housing and employer-provided housing, depending on structure

The exemption trips people up, so a few points:

  • "Owner-occupied" means the owner actually lives in the building. A landlord who owns six units and lives elsewhere is covered.
  • Exempt does not mean rightless. Your lease still binds both sides, and state law still bans self-help evictions and covers other basics.
  • Subsidized tenants often have both the RLTO and program rules. When they conflict, the stricter protection for the tenant usually matters, so keep your program paperwork handy.
  • If you rent a room inside the owner's own home, ask a tenant organization before assuming you are covered.

4. Before you sign: the lease

The lease is where most disputes begin. A few ground rules from the ordinance:

  • Some lease clauses are prohibited, such as terms that waive your RLTO rights. A prohibited clause is generally void even if you signed.
  • Late fees are capped. A landlord may charge no more than $10 a month on the first $500 of rent, plus 5% of any rent above $500. On $1,500 of rent, that is $10 plus $50, or $60.
  • Landlords must give certain disclosures and the current deposit interest information.
  • Ask for a signed copy of the lease and every rider, and keep them where you can find them.

5. Security deposits

  • The landlord must give a signed receipt naming the owner, the date, and the unit.
  • Deposits held more than six months earn annual interest at the city's published rate. For 2026, that rate is 0.01%, set by the City Comptroller each January.
  • Deposits must be held in a separate account, not mixed with the landlord's operating funds.
  • After you move out, the landlord must send an itemized statement of deductions within 30 days, and return the deposit plus interest, less lawful deductions, within 45 days.
  • If a landlord violates the deposit rules, remedies can include damages of up to two times the deposit plus interest and attorney's fees.

6. Habitability and repairs

Landlords are generally required to keep the unit in compliance with the city's building code: working plumbing, structurally sound floors and stairs, functioning locks, no pest infestations, and no exposed hazards like lead paint in poor condition. When something breaks that affects habitability, tenants generally have to give written notice and a reasonable window to fix it before pursuing remedies.

  • Ordinary repairs: written notice, then generally 14 days before remedies such as repair-and-deduct, rent reduction or lease termination.
  • Essential services (heat, running or hot water, electricity, gas, plumbing): a request to correct within 24 hours, then remedies.
  • Repair-and-deduct is capped at the greater of $500 or half a month's rent, and not more than one month's rent.

7. Heat and utilities

From September 15 through June 1, Chicago's Heat Ordinance requires landlords with central heat to maintain:

  • 68°F from 8:30 a.m. to 10:30 p.m.
  • 66°F from 10:30 p.m. to 8:30 a.m.

This applies in habitable rooms and bathrooms, regardless of the reason heat is out. Landlords who fail to comply face city fines of $500 to $1,000 per day, per violation. A landlord also generally may not shut off utilities to force you out.

8. Landlord entry and your privacy

You rent the home, and the landlord has to respect that. A landlord generally needs at least two days' notice to enter for most reasons, with exceptions for emergencies and certain urgent repairs. Entry between 8:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. is presumed reasonable, and entry outside those hours is not unless it is an emergency or you asked for it. A landlord cannot use access to harass you, and a tenant who suffers unlawful entry may be able to recover money damages.

9. Retaliation

You can complain to the City, ask for repairs, or organize with neighbors without being punished for it. The ordinance prohibits retaliation such as ending or threatening to end a tenancy, raising rent, cutting services, bringing or threatening an eviction case, or refusing to renew because of a good-faith protected act. If the landlord's action comes within a year after your protected activity, the timing creates a rebuttable presumption of retaliation, and the landlord then has to show a legitimate reason. A dated timeline is the best tool you have.

10. Eviction basics

A landlord cannot lock you out, remove your belongings, or shut off utilities to force you out. These "self-help" evictions are generally not permitted. Eviction has to go through Cook County court, starting with a written notice (the type and length depend on the reason), then a filed case, then a court judgment before a sheriff can remove anyone.

ReasonNotice
Unpaid rent5 days
Lease violation10 days
No cause, non-renewal or rent increase30, 60 or 120 days, based on how long you have lived there

If you are served with court papers, go to the court date. Missing it can lead to a default judgment.

11. Moving out the right way

Document the unit's condition before you hand back keys. Photos, timestamps, and a written move-out notice protect you if the landlord later disputes damage or delays returning your deposit. Give your forwarding address in writing, and ask for a walkthrough.

12. Habits that protect you

  • Put it in writing. A call is gone the moment it ends. An email or letter is evidence.
  • Keep copies and proof of delivery. Certified mail, email with a timestamp, or a signed receipt.
  • Date your photos. And keep the originals.
  • Pay by traceable method. And keep receipts for cash.
  • Keep one folder for the lease, riders, notices, receipts and messages.
  • Do not ignore a notice or court paper, even if you think it is wrong.
  • Ask before withholding rent. It is a remedy the law recognizes, and it also carries real risk.

15. Frequently asked questions

Does the Chicago RLTO apply to my apartment?

Most rental units in Chicago are covered. The main exemption is owner-occupied buildings of six units or fewer, though even those retain some protections.

How much interest does a Chicago landlord owe on my security deposit?

For deposits held during 2026, the required rate is 0.01%, the same minimum that's applied since 2015.

What temperature is my landlord legally required to maintain?

With central heat, 68°F during the day and 66°F overnight, from September 15 through June 1.

Can my landlord evict me without going to court?

Generally no. Self-help evictions are generally not permitted. Eviction generally requires a Cook County court order.

How much can a landlord charge for a late fee?

No more than $10 a month on the first $500 of rent plus 5% of the rent above $500.

How much notice must a landlord give before entering?

Generally at least two days, with exceptions for emergencies and certain urgent repairs. Entry between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. is presumed reasonable.

Can my landlord punish me for complaining?

Retaliation for good-faith complaints is prohibited. Landlord actions within a year after a protected act raise a presumption of retaliation.

Where can I get free legal help?

Cook County Legal Aid for Housing and Debt (cookcountylegalaid.org, 855-956-5763) and Eviction Help Illinois (evictionhelpillinois.org, 855-631-0811) are good starting points.
