Chicago Heat Law & Utility Complaints

Updated for 2026 · Part of the Chicago Renters' Rights series

This is not legal advice. This article explains general rules for informational purposes only. If you have no heat in cold weather, this can be an urgent safety issue. Call 311 to report it. If anyone is in immediate danger, call 911. Consider speaking with a licensed Illinois attorney or a tenant rights organization about your specific situation.

The short version

  • Season: September 15 through June 1.
  • Central heat minimums: 68°F from 8:30 a.m. to 10:30 p.m., and 66°F from 10:30 p.m. to 8:30 a.m.
  • How to measure: 3 feet above the floor, at least 3 feet from an exterior wall, windows and exterior doors closed, cooking appliances and space heaters off.
  • Fines: $500 to $1,000 per day, per violation. The reason heat is out does not change the landlord's liability.
  • Report it: 311 for the City, and written notice to your landlord the same day.
  • Heat is an "essential service" under the RLTO, which puts it on a faster remedy track than ordinary repairs.

1. The heat ordinance rules

Chicago's Heat Ordinance is Section 14X-8-803 of the Municipal Code, part of the Chicago Minimum Requirements for Existing Buildings, and the Department of Buildings enforces it. It runs September 15 through June 1. A landlord may turn heating equipment off or run air conditioning during that window, as long as the required temperatures are maintained.

The temperatures

Building typeWhat is required
Central heat, no central cooling68°F from 8:30 a.m. to 10:30 p.m., and 66°F from 10:30 p.m. to 8:30 a.m., for the whole season
"Two-pipe" building (one system provides both heating and cooling)The same 68/66 schedule for most of the season, with two lower windows: 64°F at all hours from September 15 until the first night outdoor temperature drops below 45°F or October 15, whichever comes first, and 64°F at all hours from the first May day the outdoor temperature exceeds 75°F until the season ends
Individual heating equipment in each unitThe equipment must be capable of maintaining 68°F under expected Chicago winter conditions

If you are not sure which type you have, ask your landlord in writing. Most older Chicago buildings with a boiler and radiators are central heat. The two-pipe category mostly matters in the early fall and late spring, when it can be warm outside and the building has not switched over.

Where heat is required

Heat must be provided in all habitable spaces (rooms used for sleeping, eating, cooking, work or recreation) and in bathrooms and toilet rooms, even though a bathroom is not a habitable space. Closets, hallways, storage areas and utility rooms have no minimum temperature. So a cold hallway is not by itself a violation, but a cold bedroom, kitchen or bathroom is.

How temperature is measured

This matters because a landlord or inspector will use the same method. Measure:

  • 3 feet above the floor
  • at least 3 feet away from any exterior wall
  • with windows and exterior doors closed
  • with cooking appliances and portable space heaters turned off

A reading taken on the window sill, next to a cold exterior wall, or with a space heater running can be waved away. Take readings the way the City would, and write down that you did.

What does not count as heat

The ordinance recognizes permanent systems: steam or hot water radiators and other hydronic systems, warm-air furnaces, and heat pumps. A landlord cannot meet the requirement with a stove or oven, a water heater, a fireplace, or portable space heaters. The City's guidance says space heaters cannot be used to meet the minimum temperature requirement, though a landlord may offer them as a stopgap while repairs happen (more on that below).

Fines and fault

Owners face fines of $500 to $1,000 per day, per violation for inadequate heat or non-working heating equipment. If the equipment fails during the heat season, the City treats that as an immediate violation. The cause does not matter: a broken boiler, a delayed part, a contractor who did not show up, or an unpaid gas bill on the building account all leave the landlord just as responsible.

The City also expects landlords to inspect heating equipment in late summer, keep windows, doors and walls in a condition that holds heat, and communicate often with tenants during repairs. A judge or hearing officer may weigh those efforts when setting a fine, which is one reason your written record of what the landlord did and did not do is useful.

If you pay your own gas or electric bill

If your unit has individual heating equipment, you may be required to pay the associated gas or electric bills. That does not shift the landlord's duty to provide equipment that works and can keep the unit at 68°F. It does mean that a gas shutoff for your own unpaid bill is a different problem from a landlord failing to supply heat.

3. When the heat goes out: the first 72 hours

The order of steps matters. Here is a practical sequence.

WhenWhat to do
Right awayTake two or three readings the correct way, in different rooms, and photograph the thermometer with a visible clock or phone timestamp. Check that your own thermostat or controls are not the issue. Dress in layers and use extra blankets.
Same daySend the landlord or property manager a short written notice (email or text, plus a letter if you can) saying the heat is out, since when, and what the readings are. Call 311 and get a service request number. Keep a copy of both.
Within 24 hoursBecause heat is an essential service, the City's RLTO summary describes a request that the landlord correct the failure within 24 hours. Put that request in writing and state the date and time you gave notice.
Day 2 to 3Keep logging readings at different times of day. Note every contact with the landlord and any repair visit. If a City inspector comes, let them in and keep the result. If the problem is still not fixed, read the remedies section below and consider talking to a tenant organization or attorney before you withhold any rent.

Staying safe in the meantime

  • Never heat your home with the oven or stove. It is a fire risk and a carbon monoxide risk.
  • If you use a portable space heater for supplemental warmth, follow the City's rules: keep it at least 3 feet from anything combustible, on a solid flat floor and not on furniture, plugged directly into a wall outlet (never an extension cord or power strip), with an automatic tip-over shutoff and a label from a recognized testing lab such as UL, ETL or CSA. Turn it off when no one is in the room, and do not use it in a child's bedroom or with a damaged cord.
  • Test your smoke alarms before running any heater.
  • If it is dangerously cold, you have infants, older adults or anyone with a medical vulnerability in the home, or you cannot stay warm, go to a warm place such as a relative's home or a City warming center, and call 911 for an emergency. Keep your receipts if you pay for a hotel or other temporary housing, because they can support a later claim for your costs.

4. Common landlord responses, and what they mean

What you hearWhat to know
"The part is on order."The reason is not a defense to the ordinance, though documented repair effort and interim measures can affect how a fine is set. Ask for a date, ask in writing, and ask what is being provided in the meantime.
"The building meets code, it is just your unit."The minimums apply to each habitable room and bathroom. A unit that is cold because of a failed radiator, a leaking window or poor insulation can still be a violation. Report it.
"It is only a few degrees under."The minimums are minimums. Two or three degrees below 68 is still below 68.
"Use a space heater."A space heater is not an acceptable way to meet the requirement. A landlord can offer one as a stopgap, and a rent credit for electricity or alternative housing may be offered as well, but that does not replace working heat.
"Heating season has not started."It starts September 15. Before that date, the minimums do not apply, though the lease may still require heat. After that date they do.
"You left a window open."This is why readings should note that windows and exterior doors were closed.
"The gas company shut us off, it is not my fault."The City's guidance is explicit that the reason heat is out does not affect liability.
"If you keep complaining I will not renew your lease."See the retaliation section below. Save that message.

5. Your remedies under the RLTO

The Chicago RLTO treats heat as an essential service, along with running and hot water, electricity, gas and plumbing. Under the City's RLTO summary, if an essential service is not provided contrary to the lease, a tenant may request that the landlord correct the failure within 24 hours and, if the landlord does not, withhold from the monthly rent an amount that reasonably reflects the reduced value of the premises. The ordinance also gives other remedies for habitability failures, including in some cases arranging the repair and deducting the cost within limits, and ending the lease. Our habitability guide covers how each works and the caps that apply.

A worked example of "reduced value"

Marcus pays $1,400 a month. His heat goes out on November 3. He sends written notice that afternoon and a second message the next day, calls 311, and the boiler is repaired on November 10. That is 7 days without heat.

  • One way to think about it: $1,400 divided by 30 days is about $46.67 a day, or about $327 for 7 days.
  • If the unit was largely unusable, a reduction near the full daily rate could be argued. If it stayed cool but not dangerous in some rooms, a smaller percentage may be more defensible. At 50 percent, the reduction would be about $163.

This is an illustration of how to reason about the number, not a formula in the ordinance. "Reasonably reflects the reduced value" is a judgment, and a landlord who disagrees may dispute it. Keep your readings, photos, notices and the 311 record, because they are the evidence for the number you chose.

Rent withholding carries risk

Withholding is a remedy the ordinance recognizes, but it is also how many tenants end up facing an eviction filing for nonpayment. If you withhold, put the notice in writing first, withhold only a defensible amount, keep paying the rest on time, and set the withheld amount aside so you can show you were able to pay. Speaking with a tenant organization or attorney before withholding is a good idea. Our eviction process guide explains what happens if a landlord files.

Other practical remedies

  • The City inspection and fines. Your 311 complaint puts the Department of Buildings on record and can lead to inspection and enforcement against the owner.
  • Your costs. If you spent money on heaters, extra electricity, or temporary lodging, keep receipts and include them in your written demand.
  • Court. A tenant can raise the condition of the unit in a housing court case, and in some situations may file a claim for damages. Whether and how to do that is worth discussing with a tenant legal aid organization.

6. Illegal utility shutoffs

A landlord may not use utilities as a weapon. The RLTO prohibits lockouts and, under Section 5-12-160, the willful interruption of utility service to force a tenant out, with or without a stated reason. Self-help eviction is illegal in Illinois even when the tenant owes rent or the lease has ended; a landlord has to go through court. Under the City's RLTO summary, a landlord who locks out a tenant can be fined $200 to $500 for each day the lockout occurs or continues.

The ordinance also lets a tenant recover possession or end the lease and recover money damages. The remedy is generally described as the greater of two months' rent or twice the tenant's actual damages, plus attorney's fees and court costs, but you should check the current text of 5-12-160 or ask a housing attorney to confirm the exact formula before relying on a specific number.

If a service is cut off

  • Photograph the shutoff, the meter, the panel or the valve, and write down the date and time.
  • If the utility is in the landlord's name, call the utility provider to report the outage and ask when service will be restored.
  • Notify the landlord in writing and request immediate restoration.
  • Call 311. If you have been locked out or are in danger, call 911.
  • Keep receipts for any food spoilage, hotel nights or other costs.

The shutoff notice duty

Under RLTO Section 5-12-100, a landlord must give tenants notice of any notice of intent by a utility provider to shut off water, electrical or gas service to the building. If a landlord knew a shutoff was coming because the building account was unpaid and said nothing, that is worth documenting. Tenants should also know about the utility-side protections available through the provider, which vary, so ask the utility directly.

A different problem: your own account

If you hold the account and fall behind on your own gas or electric bill, a shutoff by the utility after proper notice is not the same as a landlord cutting service. Contact the utility early about payment plans and assistance programs.

7. Retaliation for a heat complaint

Calling 311 or sending a written repair request is protected activity. Under RLTO Section 5-12-150, a landlord may not retaliate by terminating or threatening to terminate your tenancy, increasing rent, decreasing services, bringing or threatening to bring an eviction action, or refusing to renew a lease because you made a good-faith complaint to a government agency or exercised a right under the ordinance. If the landlord does one of these things within a year of a protected act, the law treats that as a rebuttable presumption of retaliation, which the landlord must then answer. Our entry and retaliation guide explains the timeline and evidence in detail, including a tool for building a dated retaliation record.

8. When the whole building is cold

If neighbors are cold too, the strongest move is to act together. Compare notes, have each unit file its own 311 complaint, and share your logs. Many complaints from one address are harder for a landlord to dismiss, and the City has taken landlords to court in building-wide heat cases. A tenant union or organizing group can help coordinate, and written notice from several tenants at once puts the landlord on notice about the scope of the failure. Each tenant should still keep their own record, because remedies like rent reductions are individual.

9. How to report a violation

  1. Take readings the correct way, at different times of day and in different rooms, especially early morning and evening.
  2. Log them with date, time and room (use the tool above), and note how you measured.
  3. Call 311 or file a service request online and say there is no heat or inadequate heat. Give the full address and unit and ask for a service request number.
  4. Notify your landlord in writing at the same time, referencing the specific readings, and keep proof of delivery.
  5. Be available for the inspection. If you cannot be home, ask whether someone else can let the inspector in, and tell your landlord a City inspection has been requested.
  6. Keep everything: the inspection result, any violation notice, repair receipts, photos, and every message.
  7. Follow up. If the problem is not resolved in a few days, call 311 again with your service request number and send the landlord a second written notice.

11. FAQ

What temperature is my landlord required to maintain?

With central heat, 68°F from 8:30 a.m. to 10:30 p.m. and 66°F from 10:30 p.m. to 8:30 a.m., from September 15 through June 1. Two-pipe buildings have lower early fall and late spring windows, and units with individual heating equipment must have equipment capable of 68°F.

Do the minimums apply in every room?

They apply in habitable rooms and in bathrooms and toilet rooms. Closets, hallways, storage areas and utility rooms have no minimum.

How do I measure the temperature?

3 feet above the floor, at least 3 feet from an exterior wall, with windows and exterior doors closed and cooking appliances and portable space heaters off.

Can my landlord give me a space heater instead?

A space heater can be a temporary stopgap while repairs are made, but it does not satisfy the heat requirement.

Can I withhold rent if I have no heat?

The RLTO recognizes rent withholding for failure of an essential service, in an amount that reasonably reflects the reduced value of the unit, after written notice and a request to correct within 24 hours. It carries risk, so get advice first if you can.

Can my landlord shut off my utilities to force me out?

No. Willfully interrupting utility service or locking a tenant out is prohibited under the RLTO, and landlords can face fines and civil liability. Check the current text of Section 5-12-160 for the exact remedy.

Can my landlord retaliate if I call 311?

Retaliation for a good-faith complaint is prohibited under RLTO 5-12-150, and certain actions within a year of a protected act raise a presumption of retaliation.

How do I report a heat complaint?

Call 311 or file online, ask for a service request number, and notify your landlord in writing at the same time.
