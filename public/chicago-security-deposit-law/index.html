Chicago Security Deposit Law: Deadlines, Interest & Penalties

Updated for 2026 · Part of the Chicago Renters' Rights series

This is not legal advice. This article explains general rules under the Chicago RLTO for informational purposes only. If your landlord has missed a deadline or is disputing your deposit, consult a licensed Illinois attorney or a tenant rights organization about your specific case.

At a glance

  • Itemized statement of deductions: due within 30 days after you move out.
  • Deposit plus interest, less lawful deductions: due within 45 days after you move out.
  • The 2026 interest rate is 0.01%, so interest is tiny. The deadlines and procedures are what carry the real money.
  • A landlord who violates the deposit rules can owe damages of two times the deposit plus interest, and the prevailing tenant can recover attorney's fees and costs.

1. The basic rules

Section 5-12-080 of the Chicago Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance (RLTO) sets the ground rules for any covered unit where a landlord collects a security deposit. It applies to most Chicago rentals. Owner-occupied buildings with six or fewer units are generally exempt from most of the ordinance, so check coverage with our rights checker if you are not sure.

A landlord who holds a deposit has to:

  • Give you a signed receipt. It should name the landlord, the date, the amount and the unit. If you paid electronically, an electronic receipt is acceptable. If you never got one, ask for it in writing now. It is the first document anyone will look for in a dispute.
  • Hold the money properly. The deposit goes in a federally insured, interest-bearing account at an Illinois financial institution, kept apart from the landlord's own funds. A landlord who mixes your deposit with operating money has broken the rule even if every dollar is still there.
  • Tell you where it is. You are entitled to the name and address of the bank, in the lease or in writing within 14 days of the landlord receiving the deposit. If your lease and paperwork never mention the bank, that is a red flag.
  • Pay interest. On deposits and prepaid rent held more than six months, interest is paid annually, within 30 days after each 12-month period.

These are not formalities. A missed step on any one of them can support the statutory penalty covered below, which is why it is worth checking each one the day you move in, not the day you move out.

2. How interest works, with real numbers

The City Comptroller publishes the required interest rate each year. For 2026 the rate is 0.01%. That is the same minimum that has applied for years, so the numbers are small.

DepositHeldInterest at 0.01% per year
$1,0001 year$0.10
$1,5002 years$0.30
$2,4003 years$0.72
$3,0005 years$1.50

It is easy to dismiss that as pocket change, and many landlords do, which is exactly why unpaid interest is one of the most common deposit violations. The amount is trivial. The legal consequence is not. A landlord who ignores the interest rule has violated Section 5-12-080, and a violation is what opens the door to the penalty. When a tenant is owed $0.30 in interest on a $1,500 deposit, the real exposure for the landlord is the $3,000 penalty that comes with the missing $0.30.

Each year's interest is calculated at the rate published for that year. The calculator below uses the 2026 rate as a simple estimate for any holding period. If you were a tenant in earlier years, the rate for each year may differ, and your landlord's records should show what was paid.

4. The 45-day and 30-day deadlines

RequirementDeadline
Itemized statement of damages, if deducting (with estimated or actual costs)30 days after move-out
Paid receipts for repairs, if the statement used estimatesPromptly, as the work is done and billed
Return deposit plus interest, less lawful deductions45 days after move-out
Pay annual interest during the tenancyWithin 30 days after each 12-month period
Return after a fire (building made uninhabitable)7 days after the tenant's termination notice

Both main clocks start on the day you actually vacate and return the keys, not the day your lease ends. If you left early with the landlord's knowledge, your move-out date is the date you gave up possession. Keep a dated record of when you returned keys. A text or email from the landlord confirming it is ideal.

Two practical points. First, count calendar days, and do not assume a deadline that lands on a weekend or holiday moves. Second, give the landlord your forwarding address in writing when you leave. The landlord's obligations do not disappear if you forget, but a written forwarding address removes the easiest excuse for a missed mailing.

5. A sample timeline, day by day

Maya moves out on January 31 Jan 31: Maya returns the keys, photographs every room and emails her new address to the landlord. Her deposit is $1,800.
March 2: The 30-day deadline for an itemized statement. Nothing arrives.
March 17: The 45-day deadline to return the deposit and interest. Nothing arrives.
March 18: Maya checks the dates, then sends a written demand using the generator above, asking for payment within seven days and citing Section 5-12-080.
March 26: No response. Maya files in the Pro Se Small Claims Division, claiming the deposit, interest and statutory damages. See what to do if your landlord won't return your deposit.

In that example, the landlord missed two separate deadlines. Each is an independent problem. A landlord who returns the deposit on day 44 but never sent an itemized statement for the amount deducted has still missed the statement deadline.

6. What landlords can and can't deduct

A landlord may generally deduct for unpaid rent and for the reasonable cost of repairing damage you caused beyond normal wear and tear. The difficult cases sit in the middle, so here are common examples side by side.

Usually deductibleUsually not deductible
Unpaid rent, including a final partial month you oweNormal wear and tear from ordinary living
Holes in walls larger than normal picture-hanging nail holesSmall nail holes and faded or scuffed paint from years of use
Pet urine stains or burns in carpet or flooringCarpet worn from walking, or a carpet already at the end of its useful life
Broken windows, doors, fixtures or appliances you damagedAppliances that failed from age, or fixtures that were already broken at move-in
Cleaning needed to return the unit to its move-in conditionRoutine turnover cleaning the landlord does for every unit
Missing items the lease says you must return, such as keys or provided furnitureDamage that was already present, shown in your move-in photos or inspection

The useful-life idea

Even where something is damaged, the amount a landlord can charge is generally tied to what it was worth. A ten-year-old carpet that is ruined may have very little value left, so a landlord who bills you for brand-new carpeting is charging for an upgrade, not a repair. Housing agencies and courts generally consider the age and condition of the item. Ask the landlord for the age of anything they charge you for.

What the itemized statement has to look like

The statement should list each deduction with the actual or estimated cost of repair. If it uses estimates, the landlord should follow with paid receipts once the work is done. A statement that just says "damages: $900" is not an itemized statement. Ask for the breakdown, the invoices and the dates the work was done. If the landlord never did the repair, ask what the deduction was for.

Last month's rent

Do not treat the deposit as your last month's rent. The standard Chicago lease says you cannot do that without the landlord's consent, and doing it means your final rent is unpaid, which gives the landlord a legitimate deduction and a nonpayment argument.

7. The two-times-deposit penalty

This is the part that makes Chicago's ordinance unusually tenant-favorable. Under Section 5-12-080, a landlord who violates the deposit requirements may be liable for damages equal to two times the deposit plus interest. The prevailing tenant can also recover court costs and reasonable attorney's fees under Section 5-12-180.

What counts as a violation is broad: no signed receipt, mixing the deposit with the landlord's funds, no bank disclosure, no annual interest, a missed statement deadline, a missed return deadline, or deductions for things the ordinance does not allow. Courts have often treated this as close to strict liability. In plain terms, the landlord's explanation, such as a lost check, a property manager's mistake or a late closing on a building, may not matter once a violation is shown. Results always depend on the specific facts.

How the totals add up The ordinance sets the penalty at two times the deposit plus interest. Whether a judge also orders the original deposit returned on top of that depends on whether the landlord still has it. How a particular judge adds these pieces together is a question for a court-based legal aid desk or an attorney. Treat any total, including the estimates in our calculators, as an upper-end figure rather than a prediction.

9. Special situations

  • Fire or major damage. If the building becomes uninhabitable, the deposit deadline is shorter. The City's summary puts it at seven days after the tenant's termination notice.
  • Roommates. The lease controls who gets the money. With several tenants on one lease, the landlord generally returns the deposit as one payment, and you settle shares among yourselves. Agree on one forwarding address.
  • The building was sold. If ownership changed during your tenancy, ask both the old and new owner in writing who holds your deposit, and keep the dates of every answer.
  • Owner-occupied buildings of six or fewer units. The RLTO deposit rules may not apply. State law and your lease still matter. Ask a tenant rights organization.
  • Subsidized housing. Federal program rules may add or change requirements. Check with your housing authority or program administrator.

10. Tenant mistakes that hurt claims

  • Skipping move-out photos. Without dated images, a damage deduction becomes your word against the landlord's. Our move-out documentation guide shows what to capture.
  • Treating the deposit as last month's rent. It creates an unpaid-rent problem for you.
  • Leaving without returning keys in a way you can prove. Your move-out date is the start of both clocks.
  • Waiting too long. Deposit claims are generally subject to a limitations period. Do not let months slip by. Talk to an attorney or tenant rights organization if you are not sure how much time you have.
  • Making oral requests only. Put every request in writing and keep a copy.

11. If your landlord is past the deadline

  1. Confirm your move-out date and count 45 days forward. Use the tool above to check.
  2. Check your email and mail for any itemized statement. It may have been sent to an old address.
  3. Send a written demand letter referencing Section 5-12-080 and giving a deadline. The tool above generates one.
  4. Keep records of your original receipt, lease, move-out photos and all correspondence.
  5. Consult an attorney or file in small claims if the landlord does not respond. Many attorneys take these cases on contingency because of the fee-shifting provision. The next steps are in our deposit dispute guide.

13. FAQ

How long does a Chicago landlord have to return my security deposit?

45 days after you vacate. If deductions are being made, the itemized statement is due within 30 days.

What happens if my landlord misses the deadline?

You may be entitled to damages of two times the deposit plus interest, plus attorney's fees and costs, depending on the facts.

Do Chicago landlords have to pay interest on deposits?

Yes, on deposits held more than six months. The 2026 rate is 0.01%, set annually by the City Comptroller. The amount is small, but failing to pay it is still a violation.

Can my landlord keep my deposit for normal wear and tear?

No. Deductions are limited to unpaid rent and tenant-caused damage, backed by an itemized statement and receipts.

Can I use my deposit as my last month's rent?

Not without your landlord's consent. The standard Chicago lease says so, and doing it anyway leaves your final rent unpaid.

What if I never got a receipt or bank information?

Ask for both in writing. Missing receipts and bank disclosures are independent deposit violations that can matter in a later dispute.

Who gets the deposit when there are roommates?

The lease controls. Usually the landlord returns one payment and the roommates divide it, so agree on one forwarding address.

Do I need to give a forwarding address?

You should. The landlord's duties do not depend on it, but a written forwarding address removes the easiest excuse for a missed mailing.
